# Morandi Cinematic Poster · Zeejay

Create a source-faithful cinematic photography poster in which the photograph, title, and small type behave as one composition. Build the film-poster effect entirely through typography, hierarchy, negative space, alignment, and selective depth. The result may evoke theatrical key art without representing a real film production.

Do not imitate a named film poster or copy protected title lettering. Derive an original typographic identity from the supplied scene. Do not add decorative geometric symbols or substitute collage motifs.

## Asset

- Use `assets/reference-typography-material-v1.png` only for tactile paper, restrained ink, and low-saturation material cues. Do not copy its wording or layout.

## 1. Lock the photographic plate

- For portrait inputs, default to exact vertical 3:4 unless the user requests another ratio.
- Reach 3:4 with the smallest safe crop from low-information sky, road, wall, or peripheral edges. Preserve faces, landmark identity, focal gestures, perspective, directional axes, and useful negative space. Never stretch.
- Preserve non-portrait ratios unless the user requests a new ratio.
- After cropping, treat the photograph as `LOCKED SOURCE PHOTO`.
- Outside explicit glyph masks, preserve orientation, framing, geometry, people, vehicles, signage, clouds, buildings, windows, color, exposure, contrast, sharpness, native noise, and texture.
- Do not regenerate, repaint, repair, relight, retouch, grade, blur, sharpen, denoise, age, or add a global paper effect.

## 2. Write a cinematic composition map

Before choosing words or fonts, identify:

- the hero subject, semantic keys, face and eye line, gesture, and whether the hero is frame-dominant;
- the first three things the viewer should notice in order;
- quiet fields that can hold readable type;
- silhouette edges, horizon, architecture, light beams, roads, vanishing lines, and other directional axes;
- zones of high detail, high contrast, or semantic importance that small text must avoid;
- one primary type field, one supporting type field, and one optional micro-information field;
- dominant, supporting, and accent colors by hue, value, temperature, saturation, and area.

State one short movie premise inferred from the visible scene. Use that premise only to direct mood and wording; never invent new visual events or characters.

## 3. Choose one theatrical layout archetype

Choose the archetype that best fits the composition rather than forcing a fixed template:

1. **Bottom-anchor:** hero or atmosphere dominates above; title and credits stabilize the lower field.
2. **Top-proclamation:** title or identity/byline text occupies open sky or wall above a portrait or landmark.
3. **Central-emblem:** title and hero form one iconic center through scale, alignment, and selective depth.
4. **Integrated-axis:** title follows or counters a horizon, road, facade, light beam, or perspective axis.
5. **Edge-scale:** title runs partly off-canvas to create cinematic scale while the readable core remains intact.

Do not distribute text evenly around all four edges. Create one dominant cluster, one supporting cluster, and one quiet zone. Let typography carry all graphic rhythm through scale, tracking, line breaks, alignment, whitespace, and occlusion.

## 4. Build an adaptive cinematic copy deck

Use visible scene facts, user context, and original poetic invention. The user does not need to supply real film-production information. Automatically write tasteful copy when none is provided.

Choose 3–5 of these roles according to available space; only the title is required:

1. **Title:** one original scene-derived title, usually 1–4 words. Keep it memorable, pronounceable, and exact.
2. **Identity/byline:** an author name, alias, invented character name, fictional creator credit, or non-personal phrase such as `A STUDY OF LIGHT AND STONE`. Use a supplied name when available; otherwise invent an original, obviously fictional name or omit it.
3. **Tagline:** one short emotional proposition, normally 3–10 words and no more than two lines.
4. **Atmospheric metadata:** place, weather, time, direction, coordinates, chapter number, archive code, or scene nouns.
5. **Cinematic microcopy:** an optional compact footer that may resemble a billing block through density and rhythm. Populate it with scene-derived nouns, fictional roles, fictional names, an invented imprint, or design-study language.
6. **Action marker:** a supplied date, photo date when known, fictional chapter/date code, edition number, `A CINEMATIC STUDY`, `LOCATION PORTRAIT`, or another concise anchor.

Freely invent fictional names, titles, imprints, dates, and credit-like language when they strengthen the design. Keep them internally coherent and original. Do not use the names, logos, awards, ratings, or trademarked identities of real people, studios, festivals, or film campaigns unless the user explicitly supplies or requests them. Do not add fake laurels or certification marks by default.

Across a series, vary title wording, line breaks, archetype, tagline position, and occlusion relationships. Preserve the family through visual logic, not repeated copy.

## 5. Make the title a proprietary film mark

The title is the only dominant text voice. Treat it as a designed mark rather than typed text.

### Select a genre voice

Choose one primary direction from the scene premise:

- classical serif or restrained Didone for history, memory, authority, or romance;
- compressed grotesk or slab for urban pressure, crime, action, or confrontation;
- geometric sans with generous tracking for science, distance, modernity, or quiet tension;
- humanist serif or softened sans for intimacy, travel, observation, or warmth;
- custom hybrid or carefully altered letterforms for surreal, uncanny, or ambiguous scenes.

Use at most two type families across the poster: one title family and one support family. A monospaced or ultra-condensed face may appear only inside micro-information and counts as part of the support system.

### Customize without gimmicks

- Modify 1–3 meaningful details: a cut, ligature, crossbar, counter, extension, or compressed join.
- Base the modification on visible scene structure such as a roofline, window rhythm, road, shadow, or silhouette.
- Preserve instant title recognition. Do not distort every letter.
- Avoid generic metallic effects, neon, bevels, glow, aggressive grunge, gratuitous outlines, or copied franchise styling.

### Scale and line breaks

- Let the title occupy one decisive field and read within three seconds at thumbnail size.
- For a two-line title, make the line break semantic or rhythmic; never leave a weak orphan word accidentally.
- Use tight line spacing for weight and monumentality; use open spacing only for quietness or distance.
- Align title width to a meaningful boundary: shoulder span, facade, horizon segment, road opening, credit block, or deliberate canvas bleed.
- If cropping letters off-canvas, keep the word recoverable and preserve its distinctive glyphs.

## 6. Establish an adaptive cinematic hierarchy

Start with these relative cap-height ranges and adapt to the composition:

| Level | Role | Relative height when title = 100 |
|---|---|---:|
| L1 | film title | 100 |
| L2 | identity/byline or action marker | 20–32 |
| L3 | tagline or context line | 12–20 |
| L4 | atmospheric metadata | 8–12 |
| L5 | cinematic microcopy | 5–9 |

- Make adjacent levels visibly different. Do not create many near-equal sizes.
- Keep only one L1 element.
- Use no more than one L2 cluster unless the title is intentionally subordinate to an iconic hero image.
- Do not force all five levels. Use three levels for a sparse teaser, four for most personal-photo posters, and five only when the image can support dense microcopy.
- Judge hierarchy by visual function, not literal film-industry labels.

## 7. Direct the relationship between type and image

Every text element must have a reason to occupy its location.

### Protect semantic keys

- Keep faces, eyes, key gestures, landmark crowns, primary ornaments, readable source signage, and product marks unobscured.
- Never place a letter counter, crossbar, or line of microtype across an eye or mouth.
- Preserve a clean recognition corridor around the hero's most identifying feature.

### Use negative space with tension

- Place small text only on locally calm detail with adequate contrast and breathing room.
- Let a large title bridge two zones when this connects the composition, but keep its critical letterforms over calmer passages.
- Do not merely center text in empty space. Use alignment, proximity, or directional opposition to connect it to the hero.

### Follow or counter scene forces

- Align supporting text to a real horizon, facade, curb, window rhythm, or perspective axis when this increases coherence.
- Keep title baselines optically level unless a diagonal is structurally justified.
- Use a counter-direction only when it creates deliberate tension rather than accidental crookedness.

### Create selective depth

- Place most title strokes on one depth plane.
- Allow selected hero edges to pass in front of title strokes where identity requires.
- Let selected title strokes return above calm hero areas to complete the weave.
- Use at most 1–3 meaningful occlusion moments. Too many alternating masks look fragmented.
- Keep all supporting small type fully readable; do not weave microcopy through busy subjects.

### Manage contrast locally

- Sample title colors from the photograph or use a muted complement. Reduce chroma before increasing darkness.
- Maintain title legibility through value contrast, placement, and restrained opacity—not shadows or boxes.
- Use one quiet neutral for most small type and at most one accent for the action marker.
- Test the poster in grayscale and at thumbnail size. If the title disappears, change location, value, or weight.

## 8. Construct cinematic small type

### Identity/byline

- Use uppercase or small caps with generous tracking and lighter weight than the title.
- Arrange 1–3 fictional names, aliases, or context phrases as one measured line or evenly balanced units.
- Keep this hierarchy subordinate even when it spans the poster width.

### Tagline

- Treat the tagline as a quiet voice, not a second title.
- Use one or two short lines, ample surrounding space, and no decorative effects.
- Connect it to the premise through proximity to the title or hero gaze, but do not crowd either.

### Cinematic microcopy

- Use it only when density improves the composition. It is a graphic information texture, not proof of a real production.
- Set the block in an ultra-condensed, legible support face with tight leading and controlled word spacing.
- Make fictional roles smaller and invented names or scene terms slightly larger when the renderer can preserve the distinction.
- Shape it as a compact gray texture with clean top and bottom edges.
- Align its width to the title, lower image boundary, or central axis.
- Keep it away from detailed paving, foliage, faces, vehicles, or high-contrast texture.
- Do not add logos, laurels, or rating marks unless explicitly requested.

### Action marker

- Separate it from the microcopy block with breathing room.
- Make it more visible than the microcopy but clearly subordinate to the title.
- Emphasize a supplied or invented date/code through size, weight, or tracking—not multiple colors or effects.

## 9. Use tactile material sparingly

- Build a 2–3 color low-saturation type palette: one title ink, one quiet neutral, and optionally one restrained accent.
- Apply broad paper fibers and shallow letterpress variation inside large title glyphs only when it supports the scene.
- Use finer grain or clean ink for small glyphs so they remain readable.
- End all fibers, embossing, and irregularity exactly at glyph edges.
- Do not apply global texture or force unrelated elements to share one material.

## 10. Weave the layers

Adapt this conceptual order locally:

1. untouched `LOCKED SOURCE PHOTO`;
2. title and supporting typography;
3. selected hero edges above title;
4. selected title strokes above calm hero areas;
5. fully readable microcopy and action marker when used.

Do not add boxes, panels, rectangles, circles, frames, stickers, tape, fake laurels, fake awards, decorative symbols, or unrelated graphics.
